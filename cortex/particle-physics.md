## Definition

Particle physics is the branch of physics that studies the fundamental constituents of matter and the forces governing their interactions. The field's central theoretical framework is the **Standard Model**, which classifies all known elementary particles into fermions (matter particles: six quarks and six leptons) and bosons (force carriers: photon, W/Z bosons, gluons, and the Higgs boson). The Standard Model successfully describes three of the four fundamental forces -- electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force, and the weak nuclear force -- but does not incorporate gravity.

Despite its predictive success (confirmed by discoveries including the top quark in 1995, the tau neutrino in 2000, and the Higgs boson in 2012), the Standard Model is known to be incomplete. It does not explain dark matter, dark energy, the matter-antimatter asymmetry, or why there are exactly three generations of particles.

## Key Properties

- **Fundamental particles**: 17 distinct particles (12 fermions + 5 bosons), expanding to 61 when accounting for flavor, color, and antimatter.
- **Four fundamental forces**: Electromagnetic (photon), strong (gluon), weak (W/Z bosons), and gravitational (not yet incorporated).
- **Quark confinement**: Quarks are never observed in isolation; they are bound within hadrons (protons, neutrons) by the strong force.
- **Symmetry breaking**: The Higgs mechanism explains how particles acquire mass through interaction with the Higgs field.
- **Beyond the Standard Model**: Active research areas include supersymmetry, dark matter candidates (axions, WIMPs), and quantum gravity.

## Examples

- The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN collides protons at near-light speed to probe sub-atomic structure
- Neutrino oscillation experiments demonstrate that neutrinos have mass, a phenomenon not predicted by the original Standard Model
- Quantum entanglement experiments test Bell inequalities, confirming non-local correlations between particle pairs
