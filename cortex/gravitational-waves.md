## Definition

Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime curvature produced by accelerating massive objects, propagating outward at the speed of light. Predicted by Einstein's general relativity in 1916 and directly detected for the first time by LIGO in 2015 (from a binary black hole merger). The detection opened a new observational window on the universe: gravitational wave astronomy can observe events invisible to electromagnetic telescopes, including black hole mergers, neutron star collisions, and potentially cosmic string oscillations.

The primary detectors — LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA — measure strain (fractional length change) of ~10^-21, smaller than the diameter of a proton over a 4km baseline. Signal extraction from noise requires sophisticated matched filtering and [[anomaly-detection]] techniques. Pulsar timing arrays (PTA) detect lower-frequency gravitational waves from supermassive black hole binaries by measuring nanosecond timing variations across galactic-scale baselines.

## Key Properties

- **Transverse spacetime strain**: Alternately squeezing and stretching perpendicular to propagation direction
- **Speed c**: Propagate at exactly the speed of light; confirmed by GW170817 neutron star merger
- **Multi-messenger astronomy**: GW events detected simultaneously with EM signals for neutron star mergers
- **Frequency bands**: Hz (LIGO), mHz (LISA/space), nHz (pulsar timing arrays) — different source populations
- **Detection challenge**: Strains of 10^-21 require quantum-limited measurement precision
