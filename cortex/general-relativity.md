# General Relativity

## Definition

General relativity (GR) is Einstein's geometric theory of gravitation, published in 1915, which describes gravity not as a force between masses but as the curvature of spacetime caused by the presence of mass and energy. The central insight is captured by John Wheeler's summary: "matter tells spacetime how to curve, and curved spacetime tells matter how to move." Objects in free fall follow geodesics -- the straightest possible paths through curved four-dimensional spacetime -- rather than being deflected by a gravitational force. General relativity supersedes Newton's law of universal gravitation and reduces to it in the weak-field, low-velocity limit.

## Key Properties

- **Einstein field equations:** The core equations relate the Einstein tensor (encoding spacetime curvature, derived from the Riemann curvature tensor and metric) to the stress-energy tensor (encoding matter and energy distribution). Solutions to these equations describe the spacetime geometry around massive objects, black holes, gravitational waves, and the expanding universe.
- **Equivalence principle:** The effects of gravity are locally indistinguishable from the effects of acceleration. An observer in a closed elevator cannot tell whether they are at rest on Earth's surface or accelerating through empty space. This principle guided Einstein's development of GR and connects inertial and gravitational mass.
- **Gravitational time dilation:** Clocks run slower in stronger gravitational fields. GPS satellites must correct for GR time dilation (about 45 microseconds per day faster than ground clocks) to maintain positioning accuracy. This is among the most precisely tested predictions of GR.
- **Gravitational lensing:** Massive objects bend light paths by curving spacetime, creating magnified, distorted, or multiple images of background sources. Gravitational lensing is now a standard tool for mapping [[dark-matter]] distributions and studying distant galaxies.
- **Gravitational waves:** Accelerating masses produce ripples in spacetime that propagate at the speed of light. LIGO's 2015 detection of gravitational waves from merging black holes provided direct confirmation of this GR prediction.

## Examples

- The perihelion precession of Mercury (43 arcseconds per century unexplained by Newtonian gravity) was one of the first confirmations of GR, predicted exactly by Einstein's theory.
- LIGO's 2015 detection of gravitational waves from binary black hole merger GW150914 confirmed a century-old prediction and opened gravitational wave astronomy.
- The Event Horizon Telescope's 2019 image of the M87 black hole shadow matched GR predictions for the photon ring around a Kerr black hole.
