## Definition

The habitable zone (also called the Goldilocks zone) is the region around a star where conditions permit liquid water to exist on a planet's surface, given sufficient atmospheric pressure. It is defined primarily by the stellar luminosity and the planet's orbital distance, though actual habitability depends on many additional factors including atmospheric composition, magnetic field strength, tidal effects, and geological activity.

## Key Properties

- **Inner boundary**: Set by the runaway greenhouse effect, where increased stellar radiation causes water vapor feedback that evaporates all surface water
- **Outer boundary**: Set by the maximum greenhouse effect, beyond which CO2 condensation prevents sufficient warming for liquid water
- **Stellar type dependence**: M-dwarf stars have narrow, close-in habitable zones; G-type stars (like the Sun) have wider zones at greater distances
- **Tidal locking**: Planets in the habitable zones of M-dwarfs are often tidally locked, creating permanent day and night hemispheres
- **Evolving boundaries**: As stars age and brighten, the habitable zone migrates outward, potentially rendering previously habitable planets uninhabitable

## Examples

- Earth orbits within the Sun's habitable zone at approximately 1 AU
- Tidally locked exoplanets may maintain habitability at the terminator between day and night sides (see [[tidally-locked-exoplanet-habitability]])
- Recent research challenges traditional habitable zone definitions by considering subsurface oceans and atmospheric dynamics (see [[rethinking-exoplanet-habitability]])
