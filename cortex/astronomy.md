# Astronomy

Astronomy is the natural science devoted to the study of celestial objects — stars, planets, galaxies, nebulae, and the large-scale structure of the universe — along with the physical processes that govern them. It is one of the oldest sciences, tracing from ancient naked-eye observations to modern multi-messenger campaigns that combine electromagnetic radiation, gravitational waves, neutrinos, and cosmic rays to build a unified picture of astrophysical phenomena.

Modern astronomy is organised into observational sub-disciplines defined by wavelength (radio, infrared, optical, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma-ray) and by target (solar physics, stellar astrophysics, galactic dynamics, extragalactic astronomy, cosmology). Space-based observatories such as JWST, Hubble, Chandra, and the Event Horizon Telescope have transformed the field, while ground-based facilities like LIGO/Virgo, ALMA, and the Vera Rubin Observatory continue to open new discovery windows.

The discipline increasingly relies on computational methods — from N-body simulations of galaxy formation to machine-learning classification of transient events in survey data streams containing billions of objects. This intersection of astronomy with [[data-analysis]] and [[machine-learning]] makes it a natural research domain for knowledge graph systems that must track cross-disciplinary connections.

## Key Properties

- **Multi-messenger observation** — Combining electromagnetic, gravitational-wave, neutrino, and cosmic-ray signals to characterise the same astrophysical event (e.g., neutron star mergers)
- **Redshift as cosmic clock** — Spectral redshift maps objects to cosmological distance and lookback time, enabling observations of the universe at different evolutionary stages
- **Survey astronomy at scale** — Modern surveys (SDSS, LSST/Rubin, Gaia) generate petabyte-scale catalogues requiring automated classification and anomaly detection
- **Extreme physics laboratories** — Black holes, neutron stars, and gamma-ray bursts probe regimes of gravity, density, and energy inaccessible to terrestrial experiments
- **Interdisciplinary convergence** — Astronomy draws on nuclear physics (stellar nucleosynthesis), plasma physics (magnetohydrodynamics), particle physics (dark matter searches), and computer science (data pipelines)

## Examples

- **Event Horizon Telescope** — Direct imaging of black hole shadows in M87* and Sagittarius A*, resolving structure at event-horizon scales
- **JWST deep fields** — Discovery of galaxies and supermassive black holes in the first 500 million years after the Big Bang, challenging formation timelines
- **Gaia astrometry** — Precise 3D mapping of over a billion Milky Way stars, revealing runaway stars, stellar streams, and galactic bar dynamics
- **LIGO/Virgo gravitational waves** — Detection of binary black hole and neutron star mergers, inaugurating gravitational-wave astronomy
