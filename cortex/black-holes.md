# Black Holes

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so extreme that nothing — not even light — can escape once it crosses the event horizon. Predicted by Einstein's general relativity as solutions to the field equations (the Schwarzschild and Kerr metrics), black holes are among the most dramatic objects in the universe and serve as natural laboratories for physics at its most extreme.

Black holes are classified by mass into four categories. Stellar black holes (3-100 solar masses) form from the gravitational collapse of massive stars in supernovae. Intermediate-mass black holes (hundreds to thousands of solar masses) are thought to arise through mergers in dense star clusters. Supermassive black holes (millions to billions of solar masses) inhabit the centres of nearly all large galaxies — the Milky Way's Sagittarius A* weighs approximately 4.3 million solar masses. Hypothetical primordial black holes may have formed from density fluctuations in the early universe.

The interior structure remains one of the deepest open questions in physics. Classical general relativity predicts a singularity of infinite density at the centre, but quantum gravity approaches — including loop quantum gravity (Planck stars), string theory (fuzzballs), and the Hayward regular black hole model — suggest the singularity may be replaced by a finite-density quantum object. The black hole information paradox, concerning whether information is destroyed or preserved during evaporation via Hawking radiation, continues to drive theoretical research at the intersection of gravity and [[quantum-mechanics]].

## Key Properties

- **Event horizon** — The boundary of no return where escape velocity equals the speed of light; it is not a physical surface but a causal boundary defined by the Schwarzschild radius (R_s = 2GM/c^2)
- **Singularity (classical)** — A point of theoretically infinite density at the centre, where known physics breaks down; quantum gravity theories propose alternatives (Planck stars, fuzzballs)
- **Hawking radiation** — Quantum effects near the event horizon produce thermal radiation, causing black holes to slowly evaporate over cosmological timescales; temperature is inversely proportional to mass
- **Accretion and jets** — Infalling matter forms superheated accretion disks emitting across the electromagnetic spectrum; magnetic field threading can launch relativistic jets thousands of light-years long
- **Gravitational lensing** — The extreme spacetime curvature around black holes bends light from background sources, enabling mass measurements and creating Einstein rings

## Examples

- **Sagittarius A*** — The Milky Way's central supermassive black hole, imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2022, confirming predictions of the shadow size from general relativity
- **M87*** — The first black hole ever directly imaged (EHT, 2019); the 2026 EHT campaign resolved the base of its 3,000-light-year relativistic jet just 0.09 light-years from the event horizon
- **GW150914** — The first gravitational wave detection (LIGO, 2015), from the merger of two stellar black holes approximately 1.3 billion light-years away
- **JWST early-universe black holes** — JWST discovered supermassive black holes existing in the first 500 million years after the Big Bang, challenging standard formation models
