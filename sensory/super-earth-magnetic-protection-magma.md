# Super-Earth Magnetic Protection from Basal Magma Oceans

## Summary

Published in *Nature Astronomy* (January 2026), this study by Miki Nakajima (University of Rochester) and collaborators demonstrates that super-Earths -- rocky planets 3 to 6 times Earth's mass -- can generate long-lived protective magnetic fields from a previously overlooked source: deep basal magma oceans (BMOs). Earth generates its magnetic field through a liquid iron outer core dynamo, but super-Earths may have solid or fully liquid cores that cannot produce fields the same way. The researchers discovered that under the extreme pressures found deep in super-Earth mantles, iron-rich molten rock (magma) becomes metallic and electrically conductive -- sufficient to sustain a powerful magnetic dynamo lasting billions of years.

The key evidence came from laser shock experiments at the University of Rochester's Laboratory for Laser Energetics, where the team measured the electrical conductivity of (Mg,Fe)O (a magma ocean analogue) at extreme pressures. The results showed that at pressures characteristic of super-Earth interiors, this material transitions to a metallic state with conductivity high enough to generate magnetic fields through convective motion. These BMO-driven dynamos could produce stronger, longer-lived magnetic shields than Earth's core dynamo, fundamentally reshaping how scientists assess exoplanet habitability.

The finding has broad implications because super-Earths are among the most common planet types in the galaxy, and many orbit within their stars' habitable zones. A long-lived magnetic shield is considered essential for habitability because it prevents stellar winds from stripping away a planet's atmosphere and protects surfaces from harmful radiation.

## Key Findings

- **Metallic magma under pressure**: Iron-rich magma becomes metallic and electrically conductive at the extreme pressures found in super-Earth interiors, enabling dynamo action in the mantle rather than (or in addition to) the core
- **Billions of years of protection**: Super-Earths (3-6x Earth mass) could maintain BMO-driven magnetic fields for several billion years, potentially exceeding the lifetime of Earth-type core dynamos
- **Alternative dynamo source**: This represents a fundamentally different magnetic field generation mechanism -- in the silicate mantle rather than the metallic core, challenging the "solar system paradigm" for planetary magnetic fields
- **Laser shock confirmation**: Laboratory experiments at URochester's Laboratory for Laser Energetics directly measured the conductivity transition, providing empirical (not just theoretical) support
- **Earth connection**: The BMO dynamo concept was first proposed to explain how Earth may have had a magnetic field before its inner core formed; on super-Earths, the mechanism persists far longer due to higher pressures
- **Scaling relationships**: A follow-up paper by Lherm et al. (2026) in *JGR: Planets* showed that more massive planets host larger BMOs with stronger, longer-lasting silicate dynamos

## Methodology

The team conducted high-pressure laser shock experiments on (Mg,Fe)O samples at the Laboratory for Laser Energetics, measuring electrical conductivity at pressures representative of super-Earth mantle conditions. These experimental results were then incorporated into thermal evolution models coupling core, basal magma ocean, and mantle dynamics to predict the duration and strength of BMO-generated magnetic fields across a range of planet masses. The models tracked crystallization front propagation, heat flux across the BMO, and the resulting convective vigor to determine whether dynamo conditions were met and for how long they persisted.

## Implications

As Luca Maltagliati (senior editor at *Nature Astronomy*) noted, "This paper suggests that exoplanets might not necessarily follow the solar system paradigm concerning magnetic field generation." The finding significantly expands the category of potentially habitable worlds: super-Earths that were previously considered unlikely to have magnetic protection may in fact be among the best-shielded planets in the galaxy. This has direct implications for target selection in future exoplanet characterization missions (e.g., with the Habitable Worlds Observatory). The cascading emergence chain -- high mass leads to high pressure leads to metallic conductivity leads to dynamo leads to magnetic shield leads to atmospheric retention leads to habitability -- illustrates how planetary habitability depends on interior physics far beneath the observable surface.
