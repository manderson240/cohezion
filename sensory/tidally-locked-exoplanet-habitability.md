# Tidally Locked Exoplanets: Night-Side Water and Subglacial Lakes Extend Habitable Zones

## Summary

Research by Professor Amri Wandel (Hebrew University of Jerusalem), published in *The Astrophysical Journal* (January 2026), demonstrates that tidally locked exoplanets orbiting M- and K-dwarf stars could sustain liquid water through two mechanisms: atmospheric heat transport maintaining liquid water on the permanently dark night side, and subglacial or intraglacial lakes persisting far beyond the classical outer habitable zone edge due to geothermal heating. Together, these mechanisms could increase the number of potentially habitable worlds by up to a factor of 100.

## Key Findings

- **Night-side liquid water**: Using an analytical climate model tracking surface temperature across tidally locked worlds, Wandel shows that heat transported from the day side can keep parts of the night side above freezing. This extends the inner habitable zone inward — worlds orbiting significantly closer to their star than classical models allow could still host liquid water.
- **Subglacial lakes beyond the outer edge**: Liquid water could exist far beyond the classical outer habitable zone boundary, hidden beneath thick ice layers where planetary interior heat melts ice from below. Analogues exist in our solar system: Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus harbor subsurface oceans.
- **Radiation protection**: Ice layers shield subglacial oceans from energetic UV and X-ray radiation during M-dwarf stellar flare activity, providing a safe environment during the extended periods required for organic life evolution.
- **Up to 100x more habitable worlds**: Considering tidally locked planets of M-dwarfs with an extended habitable zone from subglacial water may increase the abundance of potentially habitable worlds by approximately two orders of magnitude.
- **JWST corroboration**: The James Webb Space Telescope has detected water vapor and volatile gases in atmospheres of super-Earths orbiting close to M-dwarfs, well inside what was once considered too hot for habitability — supporting the expanded inner boundary.

## Methodology

Wandel employed an analytical climate model that parameterizes heat redistribution across the surface of tidally locked planets. Rather than full 3D general circulation models (GCMs), this approach enables rapid estimation of surface temperatures as a function of orbital distance, stellar luminosity, atmospheric thickness, and heat transport efficiency. The model accounts for:

1. **Day-to-night heat transport** via atmospheric circulation patterns
2. **Geothermal heat flux** from planetary interiors melting ice from below
3. **Ice layer thickness** as a function of distance from the star and surface conditions

A complementary 2025 study in *Nature Communications* established a framework for modeling convective dynamics in tidally locked planet mantles through laboratory experiments, showing that geothermal heating in mid-to-high latitudes creates potentially habitable areas.

## Implications

- **Redefining the habitable zone**: The traditional "Goldilocks zone" is likely too restrictive. Habitability is determined by the interplay of stellar radiation, atmospheric dynamics, and interior heat — not just orbital distance.
- **M-dwarfs as prime targets**: M-dwarf stars are the most common stellar type in the galaxy. If their tidally locked planets are far more habitable than previously assumed, the statistical case for finding biosignatures improves dramatically.
- **Observational strategy**: JWST atmospheric characterization of tidally locked worlds around M-dwarfs becomes higher priority given the expanded habitability window.
