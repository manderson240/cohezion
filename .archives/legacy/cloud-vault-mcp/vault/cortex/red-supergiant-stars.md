# Red Supergiant Stars

## Definition

Red supergiants (RSGs) are massive evolved stars (initial mass 8-40 M☉) that have exhausted core hydrogen and expanded to enormous radii (200-1,500 R☉) with cool surface temperatures of 3,500-4,500 K. They occupy the upper-right corner of the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram and represent the **final nuclear burning stage** before core collapse and Type II supernova. Despite their cool surfaces, RSGs are among the most luminous stars in the universe (10⁴-10⁵ L☉), radiating primarily in the infrared.

The RSG phase is brief — only 300,000 to ~1 million years — making these stars rare but astrophysically critical. Every heavy element heavier than iron in the universe was forged in the supernova explosion that ends an RSG's life, or in the neutron star merger that sometimes follows. RSGs are the universe's element factories.

## Internal Structure

An RSG has an **onion-shell** structure of concentric nuclear burning layers:

| Shell (outer → inner) | Fuel | Product | Duration |
|------------------------|------|---------|----------|
| Hydrogen envelope | H → He | Helium | ~10 Myr (main sequence) |
| Helium shell | He → C, O | Carbon, Oxygen | ~1 Myr |
| Carbon shell | C → Ne, Mg | Neon, Magnesium | ~1,000 yr |
| Neon shell | Ne → O, Mg | Oxygen, Magnesium | ~1 yr |
| Oxygen shell | O → Si, S | Silicon, Sulfur | ~6 months |
| Silicon core | Si → Fe | Iron | ~1 day |

When the iron core exceeds the Chandrasekhar limit (~1.4 M☉), electron degeneracy pressure fails, and the core collapses in milliseconds — triggering a core-collapse supernova.

## Key Properties

- **Largest stars by volume**: Up to 1,500 R☉ (if placed at the Sun's center, the surface would extend beyond Jupiter's orbit)
- **Catastrophic mass loss**: Stellar winds eject ~10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴ M☉/year, stripping the outer envelope and creating circumstellar dust shells
- **Surface convection**: Giant convective cells (~10-50% of the stellar radius) drive stochastic brightness variations and eject massive plasma plumes
- **Dust production**: RSGs are major dust factories in galaxies; their mass loss enriches the interstellar medium with processed material and condensed dust grains
- **Supernova progenitors**: All Type II-P (plateau) supernovae come from RSG progenitors; the explosion ejects 5-15 M☉ of enriched material
- **Luminosity variability**: Semi-regular brightness variations on timescales of 100-1,000 days from pulsation and convection

## The Great Dimming of Betelgeuse (2019-2020)

Betelgeuse (α Orionis, ~700 R☉, ~550 ly) dimmed by ~1.6 magnitudes — its faintest in recorded history. The event was initially speculated to herald an imminent supernova, but combined VLTI/HST observations revealed the true mechanism:

1. A massive convective upwelling ejected superhot plasma from the photosphere
2. The plasma cooled rapidly as it expanded, condensing into a cloud of silicate and alumina dust
3. The dust cloud partially obscured the stellar disk, causing the observed dimming

This event demonstrated that RSG mass loss is **episodic and convection-driven**, not the steady-state wind assumed in earlier models.

## Examples

- **Betelgeuse** (~700 R☉, ~550 ly): The nearest bright RSG; Great Dimming revealed convection-driven mass loss mechanisms
- **Antares A** (~680 R☉): First-magnitude RSG in Scorpius; VLTI observations resolved its convective surface
- **VY Canis Majoris** (~1,420 R☉): One of the largest known stars; extreme mass-loss rate of ~6×10⁻⁴ M☉/year
- **WOH G64** (~1,540 R☉): Largest known RSG in the Large Magellanic Cloud; see [[woh-g64-red-supergiant-mystery]]
