## Definition

Cognitive science is the interdisciplinary study of mind and intelligence, integrating methods and theories from neuroscience, psychology, linguistics, philosophy, anthropology, and artificial intelligence. Its central question is how information is represented, transformed, and used by biological and artificial systems to produce perception, reasoning, language, learning, and action.

The field has evolved through several paradigms: from classical cognitivism (mind as symbol manipulation, 1950s-1980s) through connectionism (mind as neural network computation, 1980s-2000s) to embodied and enactive cognition (mind as emergent from brain-body-environment interaction, 2000s-present). The current frontier, as identified by a 2025 NeuroAI workshop at UCSD, lies at the convergence of cognitive science and AI: building systems that not only match human performance on benchmarks but deepen our understanding of how minds actually work. This includes debates about AI consciousness, with researchers operationalizing markers from global workspace theory, predictive coding, and integrated information theory to evaluate whether large language models meet cognitive criteria for awareness.

## Key Properties

- **Interdisciplinary by design**: No single discipline owns the study of mind — cognitive science requires integration across neuroscience (biological substrates), psychology (behavioral patterns), linguistics (language structure), philosophy (conceptual frameworks), and AI (computational models)
- **Embodied cognition**: Thinking and perceiving emerge from dynamic interaction between brain, body, and environment — intelligence cannot be fully understood by studying computation in isolation from physical embodiment
- **Levels of analysis**: Marr's tri-level framework (computational, algorithmic, implementational) provides the standard analytical decomposition — what is computed, how it is computed, and what physical mechanism realizes it
- **Ecological validity**: Theories must explain cognition in naturalistic contexts, not only in controlled laboratory settings — real-world reasoning involves noisy data, time pressure, and social context
- **Cognitive co-evolution with technology**: Technologies are not passive instruments but active shapers of human cognition — what is termed "technological cognition" falls within embodied and extended cognition frameworks

## Examples

- **Working memory models**: Baddeley's multi-component model (phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, central executive, episodic buffer) explains how humans temporarily hold and manipulate information — directly informing context window design in AI systems
- **Predictive coding**: The brain as a prediction machine that minimizes surprise — this framework explains perception, attention, and learning as Bayesian inference over hierarchical generative models
- **Theory of mind**: The ability to attribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to other agents — tested in both children (false-belief tasks) and LLMs (evaluating whether models can reason about others' mental states)
- **Language acquisition**: How children learn language from limited, noisy input challenges purely data-driven AI approaches and motivates investigation of innate biases and structure-mapping mechanisms
