# Planetary Systems

## Definition

Planetary systems are gravitationally bound collections of non-stellar bodies orbiting one or more stars, including planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, and comets. Our Solar System is one example, but over 5,700 exoplanetary systems have been confirmed as of 2025, hosting over 5,800 exoplanets since the first radial-velocity detection of 51 Pegasi b in 1995. Planetary systems exhibit enormous diversity in architecture — from tightly packed systems of super-Earths in resonant chains to widely spaced gas giant configurations that dwarf our own Solar System.

The study of planetary systems underwent a paradigm shift when the Kepler mission (2009-2018) revealed that the Solar System is **not typical**: most planetary systems look nothing like ours. Hot Jupiters, mini-Neptunes, and super-Earths are the most common planet types, while true Jupiter analogs and small rocky planets in wide orbits are comparatively rare (or harder to detect).

## Formation and Evolution

### Core Accretion Model

The dominant formation theory: dust grains in a protoplanetary disk collide and stick, growing from µm-scale particles to km-scale planetesimals to planet-scale bodies over ~10 Myr. Gas giants must form before the disk dissipates (~3-10 Myr) to capture thick hydrogen-helium envelopes.

### Gravitational Instability

An alternative for massive planets: disk fragments directly collapse into gas giants under self-gravity. This may explain wide-orbit gas giants that are difficult to form via core accretion.

### Orbital Migration

Planets do not stay where they form. Disk-planet interactions drive **Type I migration** (low-mass planets spiraling inward) and **Type II migration** (gap-opening giants). The existence of hot Jupiters — gas giants at 0.05 AU — is direct evidence of migration.

### Late Heavy Bombardment

Gravitational interactions between giant planets can destabilize the system long after formation. The Nice model proposes that our Solar System's Late Heavy Bombardment (~3.9 Gyr ago) resulted from Jupiter and Saturn crossing a 2:1 mean-motion resonance, scattering the outer Solar System.

## Key Properties

- **Orbital dynamics**: Planets follow Keplerian orbits governed by gravitational interactions with the host star and each other; mean-motion resonances (e.g., 2:1, 3:2) are common in compact systems
- **Formation from protoplanetary disks**: Systems form from rotating disks of gas and dust around young stars via accretion and gravitational collapse
- **Architectural diversity**: Discovered systems range from hot Jupiters (gas giants in tight orbits) to multi-planet systems in compact resonant chains (e.g., TRAPPIST-1 with 7 Earth-sized planets)
- **Habitability constraints**: Planetary mass, composition, magnetic field, and orbital distance determine potential for liquid water and atmospheric retention (see [[habitable-zone]])
- **Tidal effects**: Close-in planets experience tidal locking, tidal heating, and orbital circularization from stellar gravitational interactions
- **Multiplicity**: Most confirmed systems host multiple planets; single-transit systems may have unseen companions
- **Host star dependence**: M-dwarf stars (most common) host close-in rocky planets; Sun-like stars host the widest diversity of architectures

## Detection Methods

| Method | Sensitivity | Planets Found | Key Missions/Surveys |
|--------|------------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Transit** | Short-period, large radii | ~4,200+ | Kepler, TESS, PLATO |
| **Radial velocity** | Massive, short-period | ~1,100+ | HARPS, ESPRESSO |
| **Direct imaging** | Wide-orbit, massive | ~70+ | GPI, SPHERE, JWST |
| **Microlensing** | Wide-orbit, any mass | ~200+ | OGLE, Roman (future) |
| **Astrometry** | Massive companions | ~10+ | Gaia |

## Examples

- **TRAPPIST-1**: 7 Earth-sized planets in a resonant chain around an M-dwarf, with 3 in the [[habitable-zone]]; the most studied multi-planet system for habitability
- **51 Pegasi b**: First confirmed exoplanet around a Sun-like star (1995); a hot Jupiter that overturned the assumption that gas giants only exist at wide orbits
- Super-Earths with magma oceans generating magnetic fields for atmospheric protection (see [[super-earth-magnetic-protection-magma]])
- Tidally locked exoplanets with permanent day/night hemispheres and potential habitability at the terminator (see [[tidally-locked-exoplanet-habitability]])
