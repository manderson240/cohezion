## Definition

Quantum mechanics is the fundamental physical theory describing the behavior of matter and energy at atomic and subatomic scales. It replaces classical Newtonian mechanics at these scales, introducing wave-particle duality (particles exhibit wave behavior), the uncertainty principle (conjugate variables like position and momentum cannot both be precisely known simultaneously), quantization (energy states are discrete, not continuous), and the superposition principle (quantum systems exist in linear combinations of states until measured).

The theory's probabilistic nature (measurement outcomes follow probability amplitudes given by the wavefunction) was philosophically contentious — Einstein's objection led to the EPR paradox, which Bell later resolved experimentally in favor of quantum mechanics over local hidden variables. Modern applications include quantum computing ([[quantum-entanglement]] enabling entangled qubits), quantum sensing ([[dark-matter-detection]] using quantum-limited amplifiers), and quantum communication (quantum key distribution).

## Key Properties

- **Wave-particle duality**: Quantum objects exhibit both wave and particle properties depending on measurement
- **Uncertainty principle**: ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2; precision in position limits precision in momentum
- **Quantization**: Energy levels, spin, and other quantities take discrete values
- **Superposition**: States are linear combinations; collapse occurs on measurement
- **Non-locality**: Entangled particles exhibit correlated measurements regardless of separation
