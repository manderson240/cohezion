## Definition

Stellar evolution describes the sequence of changes a star undergoes from its formation in a molecular cloud through its eventual death. A star's evolutionary path is determined overwhelmingly by its initial mass. Stars spend most of their lives on the **main sequence**, fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. When hydrogen fuel is exhausted, the star's subsequent evolution depends on whether it is low-mass (below ~8 solar masses) or high-mass.

Low-mass stars expand into red giants, shed their outer layers as planetary nebulae, and leave behind white dwarfs that slowly cool over billions of years. High-mass stars progress through additional fusion stages (helium, carbon, oxygen, silicon, up to iron), expand into red supergiants, and end in catastrophic supernova explosions that leave behind neutron stars or black holes. Supernovae also enrich the interstellar medium with heavy elements, seeding the formation of new stars and planets.

## Key Properties

- **Mass determines fate**: Initial mass is the single most important factor governing a star's lifetime and death.
- **Main sequence lifetime**: More massive stars burn hotter and die faster (millions of years) compared to Sun-like stars (billions of years).
- **Red giant phase**: Hydrogen shell burning causes the star to expand 10-100x in radius with a cooler surface temperature.
- **Nucleosynthesis**: Fusion in massive stars creates elements up to iron; heavier elements are forged in supernovae and neutron star mergers.
- **Stellar remnants**: White dwarfs (low-mass), neutron stars (intermediate-mass), or black holes (high-mass) are the endpoints of evolution.
