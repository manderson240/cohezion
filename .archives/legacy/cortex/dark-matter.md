## Definition

Dark matter is a hypothetical form of matter that does not emit, absorb, or reflect light, yet comprises approximately 27% of the total mass-energy content of the universe (versus 5% ordinary matter). Its existence is inferred from gravitational effects: galaxy rotation curves (stars orbit too fast for visible matter alone to hold them), gravitational lensing (light bends more than visible matter predicts), and large-scale structure formation. No dark matter particle has been directly detected.

The leading candidate particles are Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) and axions. JWST is producing the highest-resolution dark matter maps via gravitational lensing, while quantum sensor networks are constraining axion dark matter through precision spin rotation measurements. The detection challenge is that dark matter interacts only gravitationally and possibly via weak nuclear force, making direct detection experiments extremely sensitive.

## Key Properties

- **Non-luminous**: Emits no detectable electromagnetic radiation
- **Gravitationally active**: Influences galaxy dynamics, lensing, and structure formation
- **~27% of universe**: Dominant mass component; exceeds ordinary matter 5:1
- **Leading candidates**: WIMPs (heavy, weak-force interacting) and axions (ultra-light, very weakly interacting)
- **Detection frontier**: Direct (underground detectors), indirect (gamma-ray telescopes), and quantum sensors
