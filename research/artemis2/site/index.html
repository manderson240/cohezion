On 6 April 2026 four people passed 6,545 km above the lunar far side, lost contact with Earth, and watched the Sun disappear behind the Moon. Six months later the science archive is public. This is a first look at what it holds: the photos, the voices, and two small loggers in a storage locker that recorded the whole trip.

The release

Four bundles, one delay

The Planetary Data System published the first Artemis II delivery on 7 October. The SPICE bundle, which records where the spacecraft was and where every camera pointed, is still delayed. Until it lands, nothing can be placed precisely on the lunar surface.

The most notable report

Pinpricks of light

Illustration only. The flash positions are not known; these are drawn on the Earth-facing side, where the crew reported them.

“We have seen three impact flashes so far… Jeremy just saw another one.”

Reid Wiseman · 00:59:10 UTC · 7 April · during the eclipse

With the Sun behind the Moon and the cabin displays dimmed, the crew reported tiny flashes on the dark lunar surface: meteoroids striking the Moon. Asked where, Wiseman answered at 01:00: “on or a bit south of the equator and on the Earth side of the moon.” Ground control replied that citizen observers on Earth had been watching that same side.

Solid

  • Several crew members independently describe brief, white, star-sized flashes. Hansen: “a pinprick of light… no color.” Wiseman: “a millisecond… white, bluish white.”
  • They were reported on the Earth-facing side, so ground-based lunar-impact monitoring could in principle confirm them.

Uncertain

  • How many: roughly 3 to 6. Hansen later said he and Wiseman “saw two identical ones”, so at least one was double-counted.
  • When: mission control asked for flash reports from 22:33, and the next day the science team said they lacked “clarity on when the first four… occurred.” Most were probably seen around the eclipse, by the crew's recollection.
  • Where exactly: no positions were logged. A crew annotation PDF exists, and the delayed SPICE geometry will help.
  • Caveats the crew raised: an earlier report of “two meteors”, and Wiseman's own “maybe it was a priming bias”. Astronauts also see cosmic-ray flashes in their eyes; the crew said these were different.

Why the eclipse helped: no solar glare, dark-adapted eyes, and a night hemisphere seen from thousands of kilometres instead of 384,000. An eclipse is not required to see impact flashes; telescopes on Earth record them on the Moon's night side routinely.

Seen by eye

Cameras struggled with earthshine and the glow of the hidden Sun. Some of the richest data in this release are spoken.

Orion environment collection

The locker that will hold the Moon

Two pocket-sized loggers rode in bags inside Orion's storage lockers, recording every minute for 26 days. Future crews will bring lunar samples home in these lockers, so their air matters.

In flight5.1 g/m³

Mean absolute humidity in flight, the same as on the launch pad. RH stayed between 18 and 37 %; the air never came within 15 °C of its dew point.

Flyby0.24 °C

Locker E's whole temperature range across closest approach and the eclipse: no larger than any ordinary four hours of flight. A weak test, since the loggers are bagged and slow.

Splashdown+17 min

Humidity in Locker E rose 17 minutes after splashdown and reached 10 g/m³ within three hours. Locker F lagged until 01:42 UTC. Sample exposure starts in the ocean, days before handover.

Extremes27.1 °C

The archive's highest readings (27.1 °C, 78 % RH) came after the mission, during shipping. Label statistics overstate the flight environment.
