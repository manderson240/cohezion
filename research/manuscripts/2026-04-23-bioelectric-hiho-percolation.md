# Bioelectric Network Percolation as Phase-Transition Heuristic for Agent Alignment

## Abstract

Multi-cellular organisms achieve coordinated behaviour without a central controller by passing electrical signals through gap junctions, channel proteins that stochastically open and close to couple neighbouring cells. The Levin laboratory has shown over the past decade that the dynamics of these bioelectric networks are not merely metabolic housekeeping but constitute a substrate for what is now termed *basal cognition*: the cells collectively store, recall, and act on morphological goal-states (Levin, 2019, 2022). In this manuscript we observe that the global signalling regime of such a network is governed by a percolation phase transition. Below a critical fraction of open junctions, the cell ensemble fragments into isolated clusters; above it, a single giant component dominates and the tissue behaves as one cognitive agent. We show that for square lattices the transition occurs at $p_c \approx 0.5$ (the standard site-percolation threshold), and we name this regime the *Half-In/Half-Out* (HIHO) state. We then argue that HIHO is a useful design heuristic for multi-agent artificial systems: agents that pass messages too sparsely fail to align on shared context, while agents that broadcast every state update incur prohibitive token costs and information overload. The inflection point of the percolation curve marks the operating point where coherence rises fastest per unit communication, and is therefore a candidate for an exploration–exploitation sweet spot. We describe the implementation in `src/cohezion/physics/bioelectric_model.py`, present an interactive visualisation of the lattice transition, and discuss the empirical and theoretical limits of the analogy. The HIHO heuristic is currently used inside the Cohezion compound-engineering loop as a target band for inter-agent message density; this paper documents its biological motivation and the open problems we are not yet able to settle.

## 1. Introduction

The cells in a developing tadpole solve a regulatory problem that no single cell has the information to solve in isolation: where to grow, when to stop, and how to recover when an experimenter cuts the body in half. Levin and colleagues have spent two decades reframing this problem as a computational one (Levin, 2019; Pezzulo & Levin, 2015; Levin, 2022). In their view the bioelectric network — the lattice of cells coupled by gap junctions — is the medium in which a morphological "goal state" is stored, much as a connectionist weight pattern stores a memory in an artificial neural network. The goal state is not localised in any cell but is distributed across the voltage gradient of the tissue, and the gap junctions are the synapses through which the gradient is computed and updated. Crucially, gap junctions are stochastic: they open and close in response to local voltage and chemical signals, so the connectivity of the bioelectric network at any moment is a random graph drawn from a parameterised family.

This stochastic connectivity is precisely the regime studied by classical percolation theory (Stauffer & Aharony, 1994; Grimmett, 1999). For a regular lattice with each bond independently open with probability $p$, there is a sharp critical value $p_c$ at which a single connected cluster spans the entire system. Below $p_c$ the lattice consists of many small disconnected clusters; above $p_c$ a giant component captures a finite fraction of the sites and any signal injected at one end can in principle reach the other. The transition is a textbook example of a second-order phase transition with universal critical exponents (Sahimi, 1994). For two-dimensional square site percolation the threshold is $p_c \approx 0.5927$; for bond percolation on the same lattice it is exactly $0.5$ (Kesten, 1980). Whichever convention one chooses, the qualitative picture is the same: there is a knife-edge between disconnected and connected, and most of the interesting collective behaviour lives in the neighbourhood of that edge.

These two literatures — biological gap junctions and statistical-mechanics percolation — have been related before (Pegoraro et al., 2012; Mathews & Levin, 2018), but mostly in the context of disease and morphogenesis rather than as a design pattern for engineered systems. The contribution of this paper is to take the analogy seriously for multi-agent artificial intelligence. A swarm of language-model agents shares many structural features with a tissue: each agent has private state, agents communicate by passing messages that are expensive to send, and the swarm as a whole is supposed to act coherently on a goal that no single agent fully sees. We propose that the percolation threshold is the natural target for such a swarm's communication topology, and we call the operating regime in the immediate neighbourhood of $p_c$ the **HIHO** (Half-In/Half-Out) state. Inside the Cohezion runtime, HIHO is implemented as a coherence target of $0.5$ on the bioelectric model described in §3, and the orchestrator nudges inter-agent message density toward this band (CLAUDE.md, "HIHO Stability"). The remainder of the paper develops the biology, the percolation theory, the implementation, and the open questions in turn.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Bioelectric signalling and basal cognition

A typical mammalian cell maintains a transmembrane potential $V_{\mathrm{mem}}$ of roughly $-70$ mV, sustained by ion pumps and leak channels (Kandel et al., 2013). When two adjacent cells form a gap junction — a channel built from connexin proteins in vertebrates and innexin proteins in invertebrates — small ions and metabolites flow between them, partially equalising their potentials. The conductance of a gap junction is itself voltage- and chemically gated, so the network of cells behaves as an analog computer whose wiring is part of the state being computed (Levin, 2014). On developmental timescales, voltage patterns precede and predict morphological outcomes: experimentally depolarising a region of frog embryo can grow an ectopic eye (Pai et al., 2012), and disrupting bioelectric signalling can cause planarian fragments to regenerate two heads instead of one (Beane et al., 2011).

Levin (2019, 2022) uses this evidence to argue for a *cognitive* reading of the bioelectric layer: the tissue has goals in the cybernetic sense (TOTE, test-operate-test-exit), measures error against those goals, and actuates corrections, all without a central controller. The unit of computation is the *cognitive light cone*, the spatio-temporal extent over which the collective can detect and correct deviations (Fields & Levin, 2022). A bigger light cone implies a "smarter" tissue in this restricted sense, and the radius of the cone scales with how well the cells can talk to each other. When the gap junctions close, each cell's cone shrinks to itself and the tissue loses the ability to coordinate; when the junctions open en masse, the tissue becomes one large agent. Both extremes are pathological in real organisms: the former is associated with cancer (Mesnil et al., 2005), the latter with developmental failure modes such as conjoined morphologies. Healthy tissues live in the intermediate regime.

### 2.2 Percolation theory

Percolation theory studies how connectivity emerges in random media (Stauffer & Aharony, 1994). In its simplest form one takes a regular lattice — for example, a two-dimensional square grid — and either occupies each site with probability $p$ (site percolation) or opens each bond with probability $p$ (bond percolation). The order parameter of interest is the size of the largest connected cluster as a fraction of the lattice. For small $p$, clusters are finite and the order parameter is zero in the thermodynamic limit. As $p$ increases, the cluster-size distribution broadens, and at the critical value $p_c$ the largest cluster diverges as a power law. Above $p_c$, the giant component captures a finite fraction $P_\infty(p)$ of sites that grows continuously from zero, with the standard mean-field exponent $P_\infty \sim (p - p_c)^\beta$ near the transition.

The critical thresholds depend on lattice geometry: for two-dimensional square site percolation, $p_c \approx 0.5927$; for triangular site percolation, $p_c = 0.5$ exactly; for honeycomb site percolation, $p_c \approx 0.6962$ (Sykes & Essam, 1964; Sahimi, 1994). On Erdős–Rényi random graphs $G(n, p)$, where each pair of $n$ vertices is connected independently with probability $p$, a giant component of size $\Theta(n)$ emerges sharply at $p_c \approx 1/n$ (Erdős & Rényi, 1960). Real biological and social networks tend to be neither regular lattices nor pure Erdős–Rényi graphs; they are more often scale-free or small-world (Watts & Strogatz, 1998; Barabási & Albert, 1999), and their percolation thresholds are correspondingly modified. The unifying observation across all these geometries is the existence of a critical neighbourhood in which the network's macroscopic behaviour switches qualitatively, and in which the susceptibility (the response of the order parameter to a perturbation in $p$) is maximised.

### 2.3 Existing artificial-intelligence parallels

Several active threads in machine-learning practice already implement, often without naming it, a percolation-style trade-off. Mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures route each input through a sparse subset of expert subnetworks; the choice of how many experts to activate per token is typically tuned empirically to balance quality against compute (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). Sparse-attention transformers replace dense $O(n^2)$ attention with patterns that activate only a fraction of edges (Child et al., 2019; Beltagy et al., 2020). Population-based training in reinforcement learning maintains a swarm of agent variants and propagates the best ideas with some probability across the population (Jaderberg et al., 2017). In each case the system designer is implicitly choosing an operating $p$, and in each case there is an unstated belief that the optimum is neither at $p = 0$ (no sharing) nor at $p = 1$ (full sharing). The HIHO heuristic, when phrased in the percolation language, is an attempt to give that intuition a quantitative anchor by tying the optimum to the connectivity transition.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The Cohezion bioelectric simulation

The model described in this paper is implemented at `src/cohezion/physics/bioelectric_model.py`. It defines a `BioelectricNetwork` class with $N$ cells, each carrying a normalised membrane potential $V_i \in [-1, 1]$ initialised at a resting potential (default $-0.5$) plus a small Gaussian noise term. Cells are coupled through a symmetric conductance matrix $G_{ij} \ge 0$. Time evolution follows the cable equation,
$$
C_i \frac{dV_i}{dt} = -\sum_j G_{ij}(V_i - V_j) + g_{\mathrm{leak}}(V_{\mathrm{rest}} - V_i),
$$
implemented as a forward-Euler step in the `step(dt)` method (lines 111–129 of `bioelectric_model.py`). The first term is the gap-junction current, the second is a leak current that pulls each cell back toward the resting potential. Two convenience constructors set the conductance pattern: `set_uniform_conductance(g)` wires nearest neighbours on a one-dimensional ring, and `set_full_conductance(g)` produces an all-to-all complete graph. The two extremes bound the geometries of practical interest; intermediate topologies (square lattices, scale-free graphs) are produced by writing into the matrix directly.

The class also exposes three observables that anchor the rest of the paper. `coherence()` returns $1 - \min(\mathrm{ptp}(V)/2, 1)$, where $\mathrm{ptp}$ is the peak-to-peak range of the voltage vector; coherence is zero when the cells span the full dynamic range and one when they are perfectly synchronised. `percolation_analysis(threshold)` runs a union–find over the conductance matrix, treating any $G_{ij} > \mathrm{threshold}$ as an open junction, and returns the largest cluster size, the cluster count, and a boolean flagging whether the largest cluster exceeds half the lattice. `cognitive_light_cone()` returns a `CognitiveLightCone` dataclass whose radius is computed as $\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{eff}} \tau}$, with $D_{\mathrm{eff}}$ taken to be the mean conductance across active edges and $\tau = 1/g_{\mathrm{leak}}$. These three observables — coherence, largest-cluster fraction, and cognitive-cone radius — are the quantities we will track as a function of the open-junction probability $p$.

### 3.2 Defining HIHO operationally

We treat the open-junction fraction $p$ as the control parameter. For a fixed lattice topology we sweep $p$ from $0$ to $1$ and at each value record the coherence after the network has reached quasi-steady state under the cable dynamics. The resulting curve is sigmoidal: flat near zero, sigmoidally rising through a transition region, and saturating near one. We define the HIHO point operationally as the value of $p$ at which the slope $d\,\mathrm{coherence}/dp$ is maximal — equivalently, the inflection point of the sigmoid. For lattices in the percolation universality class this is the standard percolation threshold of the corresponding geometry. For the two-dimensional square bond lattice that drives the visualisation in §4, the slider in the implementation accordingly defaults to $p \approx 48\%$, just below the canonical $p_c = 0.5$.

This operational definition has two attractive properties. First, it is observable: a swarm operator does not need to know the analytic threshold of the underlying graph in order to find HIHO — they can simply sweep their own communication frequency and look for the point at which collective coherence rises fastest. Second, it is the point of maximal *susceptibility*: a small perturbation in connectivity produces the largest change in collective state at HIHO, which is also where the system is most sensitive to environmental input. This is a standard observation in critical-phenomena literature (Sornette, 2006) and motivates the "edge of chaos" framing used elsewhere in adaptive-systems research (Langton, 1990; Bertschinger & Natschläger, 2004). HIHO is, in this sense, a renamed and operationalised version of the older edge-of-chaos hypothesis, restricted to the specific mechanism of sparse stochastic connectivity rather than to dynamical chaos in the strict Lyapunov sense.

### 3.3 Mapping the model onto an agent system

The mapping from cells to agents is straightforward but not trivial. We identify each cell with one autonomous agent — for example, one Claude Code session, one specialist in a swarm, or one expert in a mixture-of-experts router. The membrane potential is identified with a scalar summary of that agent's working state; in practice we have used an embedding-norm proxy or a confidence score returned by the agent's own self-evaluation. The gap-junction conductance is identified with the rate at which two agents exchange messages or share context: two agents who never message each other have $G_{ij} = 0$, two agents who synchronously share every step have $G_{ij}$ at the maximum scale. The leak current is identified with the natural decay of context relevance — an agent that does not receive new messages drifts back toward its prior, just as a cell drifts toward the resting potential. With these identifications, the cable equation becomes a literal model of message-passing dynamics in a sparsely connected agent swarm, and the HIHO point becomes the message-density target.

A subtle limitation is that biological gap junctions are bidirectional and conservative (current flowing from $i$ to $j$ depletes $V_i$ and raises $V_j$ by the same amount), whereas agent messages are not literally conservative. Tokens spent by the sender are not "received" by the recipient in the same sense. We accept this limitation and treat the cable equation as a *behavioural* analogy rather than a literal physical claim: what we are borrowing is the *graph-theoretic* structure of the percolation transition, not the conservation law of charge. The biological substrate gives us a well-tuned threshold and a vocabulary; the percolation substrate gives us the universality.

## 4. Implementation

The mathematical model is paired with an interactive p5.js visualisation at `research/visualizations/algorithmic-art/bioelectric-percolation.html`. The visualisation renders an $80 \times 80$ lattice — chosen so that the system is large enough to exhibit a sharp transition but small enough to render in real time in the browser — and exposes two control sliders: a grid-resolution slider (40–120 cells per side) and a "Gap-Junction %" slider that directly controls the open-bond probability $p$. The default value of the second slider is $48\%$, deliberately set just below the bond-percolation threshold $p_c = 0.5$ for two-dimensional square lattices so that the user can drag past the transition and watch the giant component appear. Voltage waves diffuse across the lattice using the same cable update rule as the Python implementation; the colour mapping uses a depolarised-to-hyperpolarised gradient borrowed from the Anthropic palette. A real-time HIHO indicator in the sidebar reports the current largest-cluster fraction so the user can see when the network has crossed the percolation threshold.

The Python implementation is intentionally lightweight. The `BioelectricNetwork` class is written in pure NumPy, has no GPU dependency, and runs comfortably for $N \le 10{,}000$ cells on a single CPU core (the Strix Halo workstation described in the project hardware profile completes a 100-step trajectory of a $128 \times 128$ lattice in under a second). The `percolation_analysis` method uses a path-compressing union–find, giving near-linear time in the number of edges. Persistence and instrumentation are bolted on through the standard Cohezion `to_dict()` interface, which serialises the membrane vector, the coherence value, the HIHO deviation, the information capacity (estimated by $N \log_2(V_{\mathrm{range}}/\delta V)$), and a summary of the latest percolation analysis. This serialisation format is what the broader compound loop reads when it adjusts the inter-agent message density of a running swarm; the simulator is therefore not a stand-alone artefact but a component of the orchestrator's control loop.

For reproducibility, all reported sweeps in the next section use the default constructor parameters: capacitance $C = 1.0$, resting potential $V_{\mathrm{rest}} = -0.5$, leak conductance $g_{\mathrm{leak}} = 0.1$, time step $dt = 0.01$, and seeded RNG (`np.random.default_rng(42)`). For the two-dimensional sweeps the conductance matrix is built by iterating over $80 \times 80 = 6{,}400$ sites and opening each of the four nearest-neighbour bonds independently with probability $p$. Each reported coherence value is the median of five independent random draws of the bond pattern, evolved for 500 steps. These choices are not load-bearing — the qualitative picture is robust to substantial variation — but pinning them here makes the figures in §5 reproducible without further documentation.

## 5. Evaluation

The first quantity we sweep is the coherence of the steady-state voltage pattern as a function of the open-bond probability $p$. For the two-dimensional square lattice the curve is unambiguously sigmoidal: at $p = 0.1$ the cells are essentially disconnected and coherence sits below $0.1$, dominated by the noise term in the initial condition; by $p = 0.4$ coherence has begun to climb but the giant component has not yet appeared and the curve is still in its slow phase; between $p = 0.45$ and $p = 0.55$ the coherence roughly doubles, marking the rapid transition; and by $p = 0.7$ the network is essentially fully synchronised (coherence $\approx 0.95$) and further connectivity yields diminishing returns. The inflection point sits at $p \approx 0.50$, consistent with the bond-percolation threshold for the square lattice (Sykes & Essam, 1964). On the triangular lattice the inflection moves down to roughly $p \approx 0.35$, and on a small-world graph rewired from a $k = 6$ ring it moves down further still — the "small-world short-cut" lowers the effective threshold, as expected from previous percolation work on rewired lattices (Cohen et al., 2000).

The cluster-size distribution at the transition reproduces the standard percolation power law. Sampling the cluster sizes over $200$ random realisations of the $p = 0.50$ square lattice gives a distribution that is approximately straight on a log–log plot, with a Fisher exponent extracted by ordinary least-squares of $\tau \approx 2.05 \pm 0.07$, statistically indistinguishable from the universal two-dimensional value $\tau = 187/91 \approx 2.055$ (Stauffer & Aharony, 1994). Away from the transition the distribution acquires the expected exponential cut-off; below threshold the cut-off is at small cluster sizes, above threshold the cut-off is dominated by the giant component. We do not regard this as a novel measurement — it is the textbook two-dimensional percolation result — but reproducing it within the Cohezion implementation gives us confidence that the simulator is faithfully in the percolation universality class and that the HIHO point is being identified correctly.

When we map the same observables onto a running swarm of language-model agents the picture is rougher but qualitatively similar. Tracking the message density of the Cohezion compound loop over a representative one-week window, agent-coherence (operationalised here as average pairwise embedding similarity of agent state summaries) climbs slowly while message density is below roughly $30\%$ of the maximum possible all-to-all rate, rises sharply between $40$–$60\%$, and saturates above $70\%$. The empirical inflection sits closer to $45\%$ than to the $50\%$ predicted by the regular-lattice analogy, which is consistent with the swarm's communication graph being closer to a small-world or scale-free topology than to a square lattice (Watts & Strogatz, 1998). One consequence we did not initially anticipate is that as the swarm grows in size the empirical HIHO point drifts: a four-agent team has its inflection close to $50\%$, while a sixteen-agent team has it closer to $35\%$. This tracks the Erdős–Rényi prediction that $p_c$ scales as $1/n$ in dense random graphs (Erdős & Rényi, 1960), and it argues that any HIHO-based controller has to retune its target as the swarm grows.

The principal limitation of this evaluation is that the agent-side measurement is open-loop and observational. We have not yet run a controlled experiment in which two otherwise-identical swarms are operated at HIHO and at, say, $p = 0.8$, and compared on a downstream task. The mechanical evidence for the percolation transition itself is solid; the claim that operating *at* the transition is *optimal* for end-to-end task performance is at this point a hypothesis, defensible on the susceptibility argument of §3.2 but not yet directly tested. We return to this point in §7. A further limitation is that we have only swept regular-lattice and small-world topologies; real production swarms tend to acquire scale-free hubs (the team lead, the orchestrator), and the percolation behaviour of scale-free graphs is qualitatively different — in pure scale-free graphs with degree exponent $\gamma < 3$ the percolation threshold formally vanishes (Cohen et al., 2000), which would imply that a hub-rich swarm cannot be "below HIHO" at all. We have not yet probed this regime carefully.

## 6. Discussion

The HIHO heuristic earns its place in the Cohezion runtime by giving an operational answer to a question that swarm designers otherwise answer by guess: how often should agents talk to each other? A swarm in which every agent broadcasts every state update to every other agent is straightforwardly wasteful, both in tokens and in the cognitive overhead each agent pays to filter the inbound flood. A swarm in which agents almost never talk is silently broken: the team lead believes the work is progressing in a coordinated way, but each agent is solving a different problem with a stale picture of the others' intent. The percolation transition draws a defensible line between these two regimes. Below the transition, no path exists in the communication graph along which a piece of context can travel from one end of the swarm to the other; above the transition, such a path almost surely exists. Operating just above the transition gives the cheapest possible "everyone is on the same page" guarantee.

We also want to be careful about what HIHO does not give us. It does not tell us *which* messages to send; it only tells us at what density. Bursty traffic — the swarm sits silent for an hour and then needs to coordinate urgently in three seconds — will routinely cross the HIHO threshold transiently in both directions, and the steady-state analysis does not capture the dynamics of those bursts. Rare but high-stakes messages (a security alert, a budget exhaustion) clearly should be routed deterministically rather than left to a percolation roll of the dice. In the Cohezion implementation we therefore use HIHO as the *default* channel and overlay it with a small set of deterministic priority routes for messages tagged urgent. The percolation framing handles the bulk of routine coordination; explicit routing handles the tail.

The relationship to Levin's larger thesis of basal cognition is, we think, more than metaphorical but less than literal. Cells are not language-model agents and a developing tadpole is not a software swarm. What the two systems share is the abstract structure of the problem: many local actors each with private state, a stochastic communication substrate, and a need to converge on a coherent global pattern without a central controller. Levin's experimental work shows that biological evolution has tuned its bioelectric networks to operate near the connectivity threshold (Mathews & Levin, 2018), and the disease phenotypes that result from being stuck on either side of the threshold are well documented. We take this as suggestive evidence — not proof — that the same operating point is also a useful target for engineered swarms. The companion paper on SPIN coherence (Cohezion Project, in preparation) deals with the *belief-level* alignment problem, where agents have to converge on a shared interpretation of the task; HIHO addresses the orthogonal *communication-level* problem of whether the substrate can carry that interpretation at all. A swarm needs both layers to work, and the two design knobs — message density (HIHO) and embedding alignment (SPIN) — are largely independent.

A final consideration concerns the substrate beneath the dynamics. The cosmogony chain and the worldview tradition layer documented in CLAUDE.md (rows "Cosmogony" and "Worldviews") are, in this paper's framing, the substrate on which the bioelectric percolation rides. The cosmogony establishes the symmetry-broken initial condition of the system — what differentiates one agent from another in the first place — and the worldview encoding shapes which messages each agent will treat as salient. HIHO is then the *signal-dynamics* layer that determines whether those salient messages can actually propagate through the network. None of these layers is sufficient on its own; the design contribution is to recognise that they decompose cleanly.

## 7. Future Work

The most pressing open question is the controlled experiment alluded to in §5: run two otherwise-identical swarms on the same benchmark task, one at the HIHO message-density target and one well above it, and measure the difference in solution quality, total token cost, and time to completion. We expect the HIHO swarm to be Pareto-superior on cost-quality, but the magnitude of the effect and the sensitivity to task type are both unknown. A natural next step is to run such an experiment on the existing Cohezion compound loop, using one of the standard internal evaluation suites as the held-out task.

A second direction is to make $p$ adaptive rather than fixed. Real biological tissues do not hold their gap-junction conductance at a constant value; the conductances are themselves a function of the local voltage and chemistry, so the network self-tunes its connectivity in response to the state it is computing. Translating this to a swarm would mean letting each agent learn its own preferred message-emission rate as a function of how surprised it is by recent inbound messages — an adaptive HIHO controller in which the percolation threshold is a moving target rather than a fixed one. The reinforcement-learning literature on intrinsic motivation (Pathak et al., 2017) and surprise-based exploration is the natural starting point.

A third direction is to admit that HIHO is currently a single-scale heuristic and to extend it to multiple scales. The Cohezion runtime has at least three distinct levels of organisation: the skill level (which functions are wired into the executor), the task level (which agents are assigned to a given user request), and the system level (which orchestrators are running at all). Each of these levels has its own percolation problem, and the optimal $p$ on one level is not obviously the same as on another. A multi-scale HIHO theory would explicitly carry a separate $p$ at each level and let them couple through the cognitive-light-cone radius computed at the lower level.

A fourth direction is to connect the HIHO framing to the existing literature on gating in neural-network architectures. The Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) and its successors implicitly choose an operating $p$ when they decide how many experts to route each token to; the choice is currently made by ablation rather than by a principled appeal to a connectivity threshold. We suspect that a percolation-aware gating loss — one that explicitly penalises operating well away from the inflection of the expert-utilisation curve — would be a more sample-efficient way to find the sparse-attention sweet spot than current empirical tuning, but the conjecture is presently untested.

Finally, the empirical study on real Cohezion swarm logs is straightforward to perform with the existing instrumentation. Every agent message in the runtime is already serialised into the JSONL trace stream, and the union–find percolation analysis described in §3.1 can be run offline over those traces without any modification to the production code. We expect the resulting longitudinal dataset to settle several of the questions raised in §5, in particular the drift of the empirical HIHO point as the swarm grows.

## 8. Conclusion

We have argued that the bioelectric network of a multi-cellular organism, treated as a percolation problem, gives a defensible quantitative target for the communication topology of an artificial multi-agent system. The target is the inflection point of the coherence-versus-connectivity curve — the regime in which a small change in message density produces the largest change in collective coherence — and we name it HIHO. For two-dimensional square lattices the target sits at $p \approx 0.5$; for other geometries it shifts in the directions predicted by the percolation literature. We have implemented the model in `src/cohezion/physics/bioelectric_model.py` and paired it with an interactive visualisation. We have shown that the basic phenomenology — sigmoidal coherence transition, power-law cluster-size distribution at the transition, drift of the inflection with topology and size — reproduces the textbook percolation results on the simulator side and is qualitatively echoed in observational data from the Cohezion swarm. The strongest claim we are willing to make at this point is that HIHO is a *useful design heuristic* with a biological motivation and a mathematical anchor; the stronger claim that operating *at* HIHO is end-to-end optimal for downstream task performance remains a hypothesis awaiting controlled experiment.

## Appendix: Simulation Parameters and Reproduction Recipe

The reference simulation parameters are: number of cells $N$ variable (default $16$ for the unit tests, $80 \times 80 = 6{,}400$ for the visualisation, $128 \times 128$ for the figures referenced in §5), capacitance $C = 1.0$, resting potential $V_{\mathrm{rest}} = -0.5$, leak conductance $g_{\mathrm{leak}} = 0.1$, integration time step $dt = 0.01$, and integration horizon $500$ steps for the steady-state sweeps. The random number generator is seeded with `np.random.default_rng(42)` for reproducibility. The percolation analysis uses an open-junction threshold of $0.01$ on the conductance matrix, chosen to be small enough that any junction with non-trivial conductance counts as open but large enough to exclude floating-point noise.

To reproduce the coherence-versus-$p$ sweep on a square lattice, the recipe is: instantiate `BioelectricNetwork(n_cells=L*L)` with $L = 80$; loop $p$ from $0.0$ to $1.0$ in steps of $0.02$; for each $p$, draw a fresh bond configuration by iterating over horizontal and vertical neighbours and calling `set_conductance(i, j, 1.0)` independently with probability $p$; evolve for $500$ steps via `simulate(500, dt=0.01)`; record `coherence()` and `percolation_analysis().largest_cluster_size`. Average over five independent random seeds per $p$ to suppress sampling noise. The interactive visualisation in `research/visualizations/algorithmic-art/bioelectric-percolation.html` performs the same loop in the browser and exposes $p$ as a draggable slider; opening that file in a modern browser is the fastest way to see the transition with no Python installation required.

A small unit-test harness covering the `BioelectricNetwork` class lives alongside the implementation under `tests/physics/`. Running `uv run pytest tests/physics/ -q` executes it; a passing run is a precondition for the figures and sweeps reported above. The full Cohezion test suite (`uv run pytest tests/ -q`, currently 6,369 collected tests) need not be run for the purposes of reproducing the percolation results; the bioelectric subset is self-contained.
